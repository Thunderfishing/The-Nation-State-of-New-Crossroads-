## Section 0: Interdependencies (Informative)

> **Where this act sits in the system.** The Energy Act 5.2 §4 establishes the waste disposition target at maturity (2075): ~179M tonnes/year total, **79% recycled** (~141M t), **15% WTE** (~26M t), **6% landfill** (~11M t, inert only). At founding (2025), the inherited Soviet system generates ~145M tonnes/year at a ~35% recycling rate — most waste is landfilled. This act governs the 50-year transition from landfill-dominant to recycling-dominant, establishes *how* the 79% target is achieved, what happens to the WTE ash, and who audits the boundary between the recycling stream and the combustion stream. The Energy Act owns the WTE plants. This act owns everything upstream (collection, sorting, recycling) and the ash streams downstream.

> The Infrastructure Act 3.3 provides the **CO2 pipeline network** (~4,000 km) that this act's carbonation-aggregate industry depends on. The Industry Act 5.2 provides the **hempcrete manufacturing base** that absorbs the treated ash as aggregate filler. The Housing Act 2.7 creates the **Durahome demand** (~500,000 homes/year) that consumes the hempcrete. The waste system feeds the housing system feeds the construction system — a closed loop.

## Section 1: Principles

### 1.1 The Waste Hierarchy (binding, entrenched as Floor 6A)

In order of priority, all waste policy shall prefer:

1. **Prevent** — reduce waste generation at source (packaging standards, durability requirements, right-to-repair)
2. **Reuse** — extend product life through repair, refurbishment, resale
3. **Recycle** — recover materials for reprocessing into new products
4. **Recover energy** — combust non-recyclable residuals in WTE plants (Energy Act §4)
5. **Sequester** — vitrify hazardous residuals and use as repository sealing material
6. **Landfill** — inert materials only, as the last resort

No waste stream may bypass a higher-priority step to reach a lower one. Recyclable material may not be sent to WTE. Combustible material may not be sent to landfill. **The hierarchy is justiciable** — any citizen may petition the regional court (Sovereign Functions Act §4.1) if a waste operator violates the priority order.

### 1.2 The Zero-Subsidy Recycling Advantage

Recycling co-ops and FCLs receive the most favourable economic treatment of any industry in the nation — **not through subsidy, but through cost elimination:**

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Profit tax** | **Zero.** Recycling co-ops and FCLs pay no profit tax at any tier. The 5% co-op rate does not apply. The purpose is material recovery, not revenue extraction |
| **Utilities** | **At cost.** Electricity, water, and gas at generation/treatment cost — not the access-fee rate (155% of maintenance). The recycling plant's power bill is the lowest in the industrial economy |
| **Loan rate** | **Bottom of the service-sector band** — reference + 0.5% (~4%). The cheapest capital available to any enterprise. CCIF access at standard terms for qualifying Tier 2+ co-ops |
| **Property tax** | **Zero on the processing facility.** Regional/municipal property tax does not apply to recycling facilities (Municipal & Regional Funding Act exemption) |
| **Access-fee discount** | **80% co-op discount** on infrastructure access fees for transport of recyclable materials (same as all co-ops, but applied to the specific freight — recycling transport is the cheapest freight in the system) |

> **Why this isn't a subsidy.** A subsidy is a payment from the state to the operator. This is a **cost elimination** — the state simply doesn't charge the recycling industry what it charges everyone else. No money flows from Treasury. The recycler's advantage comes from what it *doesn't pay*, not from what it *receives*. The fiscal impact is a small reduction in tax and fee revenue (~$500M–$1B/year forgone), vastly offset by the avoided cost of landfill, the extended resource base, and the material inputs to the hempcrete and aggregate industries.

### 1.3 State Ownership and Co-op Operation

Waste collection infrastructure (bins, trucks, transfer stations, MRFs — Material Recovery Facilities) is **state-owned, co-op-operated** — the same asset/operator separation as the Utilities Act §1.4, Energy Act, and Infrastructure Act.

- **The state owns** the physical infrastructure — trucks, bins, sorting facilities, transfer stations
- **Co-op concessions operate** the collection and sorting, paid on a performance basis with penalties for contamination and bonuses for recovery rates exceeding targets
- **The NEB oversees** (waste falls under the energy/environment portfolio) — it does not operate. The NEB sets standards, audits performance, and publishes recovery rates. The co-ops do the work
- **Contamination verification.** The NEB shall establish and publish **standardized, objective verification protocols** for measuring incoming stream contamination at transfer stations — specifying sampling methodology, frequency, measurement equipment, and reporting format. Contamination data from these protocols is the **sole basis** for performance-contract penalties and for the Source-Contamination Exemption (below). No penalty or exemption may rest on subjective assessment or unstandardized measurement
- **Regional Boards coordinate** collection schedules, facility siting, and inter-municipal logistics

**Source-Contamination Exemption Clause.**

Co-op concession performance contracts include contamination-rate targets — the co-op is penalized if its sorted output exceeds baseline contamination levels (e.g., >3% non-recyclable material in the recyclable stream). This is fair when the co-op controls sorting quality. It is **unfair when the incoming municipal stream is degraded** beyond the co-op's sorting capacity — a large construction project dumping unsorted debris, a commercial district changing waste composition, or a seasonal tourism surge overwhelming sorting lines.

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** | The NEB publishes a **quarterly municipal contamination baseline** for each collection zone — the expected contamination rate in the incoming stream based on the zone's composition (residential, commercial, industrial, mixed) |
| **Exemption trigger** | If incoming contamination in a collection zone exceeds the published baseline by **more than 15%** in any reporting period, the co-op's contamination penalty is **suspended** for that zone and that period |
| **Investigation** | Suspension triggers an **NEB investigation of the source** — the municipality, waste generator, or commercial district responsible for the degraded stream. The co-op is not the subject of the investigation; it is the reporter |
| **Co-op obligation during exemption** | The co-op continues processing to the best of its sorting capacity. It is not excused from sorting — only from the penalty for sorting results it cannot control. Material that cannot be sorted at the elevated contamination level is diverted to WTE (§3), not to landfill |
| **Duration** | The exemption lasts until the incoming contamination returns to within 15% of baseline for two consecutive reporting periods, OR until the NEB investigation identifies and remedies the source |

*Why this matters.* Without the exemption, a co-op facing a contaminated incoming stream has two bad choices: spend unsustainable amounts on sorting to avoid the penalty, or accept the penalty and lose its performance bonus. Both weaken the co-op. The exemption creates a third option: process as best you can, report the source problem, and let the NEB fix the upstream failure. The co-op's incentive stays aligned — they still want clean streams (more recovery = more revenue) — they just aren't punished for someone else's failure

## Section 2: The Recycling Industry

### 2.1 Material Recovery Targets

> **Founding condition.** The old regime landfilled the majority of its waste. Formal recycling was minimal — perhaps ~35% nationally, mostly metals recovery from industrial streams. The targets below are what the new system builds toward, not what exists at founding. The 2025 column is the first-year target after co-op conversion of inherited waste operations; the 2050 and 2075 columns are the mature targets.

| Material stream | 2025 target | 2050 target | 2075 target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Metals (ferrous + non-ferrous) | 85% | 92% | 95% | Magnetic and eddy-current separation at MRFs. Domestic smelter demand is permanent |
| Glass | 80% | 88% | 92% | Cullet demand from domestic glass co-ops |
| Paper/cardboard | 75% | 85% | 90% | De-inking and repulping |
| Plastics (by type) | 50% | 70% | 80% | The hardest stream — chemical recycling for mixed plastics (CCIF-funded R&D) |
| Organic/food waste | 70% | 85% | 90% | Composting and anaerobic digestion → biogas (supplements geothermal heating) |
| Textiles | 40% | 60% | 75% | Fiber recovery for hemp blending |
| Electronics (e-waste) | 60% | 80% | 90% | Precious metal and rare-earth recovery — feeds the Frostpeak processing chain |
| Construction/demolition | 80% | 90% | 95% | Hempcrete is inherently recyclable (hemp fiber + lime binder can be separated). Pre-revolution concrete/rebar is the challenge |
| **Weighted average** | **~70%** | **~79%** | **~85%+** | The 79% in the Energy Act is the 2050 steady-state target. By 2075 the system should exceed it |

### 2.2 Recycling Co-op Structure

Recycling co-ops form as standard FCLs (FCL Act) with the §1.2 advantages above. They are organized by material stream — a metals recycling co-op, a plastics co-op, a glass co-op — because the processing technology differs by material.

- **Mandatory-service apprentices** (Education Act §4) serve in recycling co-ops alongside all other industrial placements. Sorting, processing, equipment maintenance, and logistics are credentialed trades
- **CLS student-venture liaisons** (Credit Union Act §5.3) connect schools with recycling co-ops for the student-venture programme — a 14-year-old's recycling micro-venture gets the same mentorship as any other trade
- **CCIF access** for Tier 2+ recycling co-ops at standard terms — the capital pool funds processing equipment, chemical recycling R&D, and facility expansion

### 2.3 Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

Manufacturers and importers are responsible for the end-of-life recovery of their products:

- **Packaging EPR:** every product sold in New Crossroads must have a published recovery plan for its packaging. The manufacturer funds collection and processing through a per-unit levy paid to the relevant recycling co-op. Rates set by the NEB, published annually
- **Electronics EPR:** manufacturers fund e-waste collection and processing. Precious-metal and rare-earth recovery revenue offsets the cost — e-waste recycling is often profitable
- **Vehicle EPR:** the EV industry (Industry Act §2.13) funds end-of-life battery recovery and vehicle disassembly. Battery minerals (lithium, cobalt) return to the battery supply chain
- **Textile EPR:** fast fashion imports face a per-unit recovery levy. Domestic hemp textiles are inherently recyclable and exempt

### 2.4 Right to Repair

All durable goods sold in New Crossroads must be **repairable by the owner or any co-op repair service:**

- Manufacturers must publish repair manuals and schematics
- Replacement parts must be available at reasonable cost for a minimum of 10 years from last unit sold
- Software locks that prevent third-party repair are prohibited
- Warranty may not be voided by third-party repair

### 2.5 Commodity Floor-Price Stabilizer

Recycled-commodity markets (scrap metal, recycled PET, waste paper, cullet) are globally volatile — scrap steel can swing 40% in a single year, tracking virgin commodity prices that have nothing to do with domestic recycling efficiency. A recycling co-op with zero tax and generation-cost power still fails if its output price drops below cost for two consecutive quarters. China's National Sword policy (2018) proved this worldwide — when scrap prices crashed, recycling operations with healthy fundamentals closed because the output was temporarily worthless.

**The CCIF Commodity Stabilization Facility.**

The CCIF (FCL Act §3.8) establishes a **commodity stabilization window** for designated recovered materials:

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Designated materials** | Scrap steel, scrap aluminium, scrap copper, recycled PET, recycled HDPE, waste paper/cardboard, cullet (glass). The NEB designates materials and reviews the list annually |
| **Floor price** | Set by the NEB at the **weighted-average break-even cost** of the nation's recycling co-ops for that material — calculated from audited operating costs at generation-cost utilities and zero tax. Published quarterly. The floor is not a subsidy price — it is the real cost of processing at New Crossroads' structural advantages |
| **Trigger** | When the spot market price for a designated material falls **below the published floor for 30 consecutive days**, the stabilization window opens |
| **Mechanism** | The CCIF purchases output from recycling co-ops at the floor price and **holds inventory** until the market recovers above the floor + 10% (the release threshold). The CCIF sells the inventory at market, recovering its outlay. The facility is **self-liquidating** — commodity prices are cyclical, and the CCIF buys low and sells on the recovery |
| **Funding** | The CCIF's existing capital pool (~$991B, scaling to ~$3T+). The commodity stabilization draw is capped at **2% of CCIF assets** in any given year — enough to absorb a severe global price crash without endangering the pool's lending capacity |
| **Duration cap** | The stabilization window may remain open for a maximum of **18 months** per material per cycle. If the market has not recovered above the floor after 18 months, the NEB reviews the floor price — it may be structurally too high, and the co-ops need to adjust costs rather than rely on indefinite stabilization |
| **Storage** | CCIF inventory holding facilities (warehouses, staging yards, covered storage for purchased commodities) receive the **same zero-property-tax and at-cost utility status** as recycling processing plants (§1.2). Municipal transfer stations shall **reserve zoned land for emergency material staging** — pre-designated, permitted, and available on 48 hours' notice when the stabilization window opens. A floor-price buy is useless without somewhere to put 50,000 tonnes of scrap |

*Why the CCIF and not the SWF.* The SWF may never fund operations (Sovereign Functions Act §5.4 — absolute bar). Commodity stabilization is operational support — buying output from working co-ops to keep them working. The CCIF exists precisely to support co-op viability through difficult periods. The stabilization facility is a natural extension of that mandate.

*Why this isn't a price-support scheme.* A price support pays above-market prices indefinitely — distorting signals and creating dependency. The CCIF stabilization facility buys at cost (not above market), holds temporarily, and sells at market on recovery. It smooths the cycle rather than overriding it. If a material is genuinely uneconomic to recycle (the floor price exceeds the long-run market price), the 18-month cap forces a structural reassessment rather than permanent subsidy.

*What this protects.* The zero-tax, generation-cost-utility advantage makes New Crossroads recyclers the lowest-cost processors on Earth. But "lowest cost" doesn't mean "profitable at any output price." A global commodity crash that pushes scrap aluminium from $1,500/tonne to $600/tonne makes even the cheapest recycler unprofitable. The stabilization facility bridges that gap — keeping the co-ops operational, the workers employed, and the recycling stream flowing while the market recovers. Without it, a 6-month price crash could close co-ops that took years to build, destroying capacity that takes years to rebuild.

## Section 3: WTE Ash Management — The Closed Loop

### 3.1 Ash Production and Classification

The 5 GW WTE fleet (Energy Act §4) combusts ~26M tonnes/year of non-recyclable waste, producing approximately:

| Ash type | ~Volume | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| **Bottom ash (aggregate-grade)** | ~4–5M t/yr | **Construction material** — after metal screening and size grading. Non-hazardous. Replaces quarried aggregate |
| **Bottom ash (metal fraction)** | ~1–1.5M t/yr | **Recyclable metal** — ferrous and non-ferrous. Returns to domestic smelters as feedstock |
| **Fly ash (treatable)** | ~0.5–0.8M t/yr | **Pozzolanic additive** — after heavy-metal stabilization. Used in hempcrete, CO2-cured aggregate, and road base |
| **Fly ash (hazardous residual)** | ~0.2–0.5M t/yr | **Vitrification feedstock** — melted to inert glass, used as nuclear repository sealing material (Energy Act §4A) |

### 3.2 Bottom Ash → Construction Aggregate

Bottom ash (after metal extraction and size screening) is a proven construction aggregate in the Netherlands, Denmark, and Japan. In New Crossroads it feeds three markets:

**Hempcrete filler.** Standard hempcrete is hemp shiv + lime binder. Adding screened bottom ash as a partial aggregate substitute (~15–20% by volume) increases thermal mass and compressive strength while diverting ~1M t/yr from landfill into Durahomes. The NEB sets composition standards; the Housing Act §2.3 material specifications incorporate ash-blend hempcrete as a qualifying formulation.

**CO2-cured aggregate blocks.** The CO2 pipeline network (Infrastructure Act §3.8, ~4,000 km) delivers captured CO2 to aggregate curing facilities where bottom ash is formed into blocks and cured with CO2 — permanently mineralizing the carbon into calcium carbonate. The block is a construction material AND a carbon sink. Each tonne of ash cured with CO2 sequesters ~0.1–0.2 tonnes of CO2 permanently.

**Road base and fill.** Screened bottom ash meeting NInfraB specifications is approved for use as road sub-base, rail ballast supplement, and structural fill. The Infrastructure Act's maintenance programme creates permanent demand.

### 3.3 Metal Recovery from Ash

The ~1–1.5M t/yr metal fraction extracted from bottom ash contains:

- Ferrous metals (steel, iron) → domestic steel co-ops
- Non-ferrous metals (aluminium, copper, zinc, brass) → domestic smelters
- Precious metals (trace gold, silver from e-waste combustion residuals) → refining

This stream generates **~$500M–$1B/year in recovered metal revenue** — which partially offsets WTE operating costs and demonstrates that "waste" is a misnamed material stream.

### 3.4 Fly Ash → Pozzolanic Additive

Treated fly ash (heavy metals stabilized through chemical fixation or washing) is a proven pozzolanic additive — it reacts with lime to form cite compounds. Uses:

- **Hempcrete enhancement** — 5–10% fly ash by binder weight improves moisture resistance and compressive strength
- **CO2-cured products** — fly ash is even more reactive with CO2 than bottom ash. The carbonation is faster and more complete
- **Soil amendment** — pH-neutralized fly ash (after heavy-metal removal) can improve soil structure in degraded agricultural land

**Residential safety standard.** Fly ash incorporated into residential construction materials (hempcrete, aggregate blocks, interior finishes) shall meet **NEB indoor toxicity and non-leaching standards** — published, testable, and enforced through the Housing Act §2.3 material-specification process. Non-conforming fly ash is **restricted to industrial infrastructure applications** (road base, rail ballast, commercial foundations) **or vitrification** (§3.5). No fly ash enters a Durahome without passing the residential standard. The NEB publishes the testing protocol; the housing co-op's material supplier certifies compliance; the Regional Board spot-audits

### 3.5 Hazardous Residual → Repository Sealing

The ~0.2–0.5M t/yr of hazardous fly ash residual (concentrated heavy metals, dioxin-bound particulates after scrubbing) is the system's irreducible toxic fraction. It cannot be recycled, cannot be landfilled, and cannot be released.

**Vitrification.** The residual is melted at ~1,400°C into an inert glass matrix — the same vitrification technology used for high-level nuclear waste worldwide. The glass is chemically stable for geological timescales. Heavy metals are locked in the glass structure and cannot leach.

**Repository sealing.** The vitrified glass blocks become **physical sealing material for the nuclear spent-fuel repository** (Energy Act §4A, Frostpeak repository SMR). The repository needs engineered barriers between waste canisters and the host rock — bentonite clay, concrete, and vitrified waste glass are the standard barrier materials. The WTE hazardous residual, vitrified to glass, serves as repository backfill — sealing the worst chemical waste inside the vault that already holds the worst radiological waste.

**The closed loop:** non-recyclable waste → WTE combustion → energy (5 GW) → ash → metal recovery + construction aggregate + repository sealing. Nothing is landfilled. Nothing is released. The waste hierarchy terminates in the most secure containment the nation possesses.

## Section 4: The Dumpster Diver Licence Programme

### 4.1 Purpose and Design

The waste hierarchy depends on correct sorting — recyclable material entering the recycling stream, not the WTE or landfill streams. The primary enforcement mechanism is the co-op concession's performance contract (§1.3). The **secondary** enforcement mechanism is 25,000 licensed dumpster divers — a decentralized, profit-motivated, informal-economy audit function that no bureaucracy can replicate.

### 4.2 The Licence

**25,000 Dumpster Diver Licences** issued nationally by the NEB, allocated regionally by population. Illustratively: ~1,250 per region, ~1 per 6,000 citizens, ~1 per 250 commercial/industrial waste generators.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Eligibility** | Any citizen aged 18+. No educational requirement. No co-op membership required. **Priority to informal-economy citizens** — the licence is designed for people who already do this work without legal protection |
| **Duration** | 3 years, renewable. Non-transferable. Revocable for cause (safety violations, trespass beyond scope, theft of non-waste items) |
| **Cost** | $0. The licence is free. The diver's income comes from what they recover |
| **PPK attribute** | The licence is a PPK-verified credential (§5.1) — verifiable by any business or law enforcement officer via a standard PPK check. No identity disclosure beyond "this person holds a valid diver licence" |

### 4.3 Legal Protections

**The diver is protected:**

- **Trespass immunity** for commercial and industrial waste areas (dumpsters, loading docks, waste collection points, transfer stations). The licence grants **right of access to waste receptacles and designated waste areas** during business hours and during the 2-hour window after business closure. Residential waste is excluded — private homes are not within scope
- **No prosecution** for possession of discarded commercial/industrial materials recovered from waste receptacles. If it's in the dumpster, it's waste. If it's waste, the diver may take it
- **Whistleblower protection** (Sovereign Functions Act §3.5 pattern). A diver who reports a waste-hierarchy violation (recyclable material in the WTE stream, hazardous material in the general stream) is protected from retaliation by the waste generator or the collection co-op

**The business is protected:**

- **Liability shield.** A commercial or industrial entity whose waste area is accessed by a licensed diver is **not liable** for injury to the diver arising from the diver's activity. The diver accepts the physical risk. The licence states this explicitly. The business need not modify its waste area, provide safety equipment, or supervise the diver
- **No duty to sort for the diver.** The business's obligation is to sort waste correctly for the collection co-op (§2.1 targets). The diver benefits from the business's failures — incorrectly sorted recyclables that the diver recovers are the diver's income AND the business's audit signal
- **Good-faith safe harbour.** A business that permits diver access in good faith and in accordance with the licence terms cannot be held liable for any claim arising from the diver's activity. The licence is the legal barrier, not the business's decision

### 4.4 The Audit Function

**The diver is simultaneously entrepreneur, auditor, and whistleblower:**

- **Entrepreneur:** the diver keeps everything they recover. Metals go to recycling co-ops (who buy at market less processing cost). Reusable goods go to resale. Electronics go to e-waste processors. The diver's income is entirely market-driven — the more the waste system fails to sort correctly, the more the diver earns
- **Auditor:** a diver who consistently finds recyclable copper, aluminium, or e-waste in a business's general waste bin is evidence that the business is violating the waste hierarchy (§1.1). The diver may report this to the NEB or the Regional Board. The report triggers an inspection of the waste generator's sorting practices
- **Whistleblower:** a diver who discovers hazardous waste (chemicals, medical waste, radioactive material) in a general waste stream reports it immediately. Hazardous waste violations are prosecutable by the Special Auditors (Sovereign Functions Act §3.3). The diver's report is protected under whistleblower provisions

**The incentive alignment is self-reinforcing:** if the recycling system works perfectly — every business sorts correctly, every MRF captures everything, every material reaches its highest-value destination — the divers find nothing and earn nothing. The programme naturally shrinks as the system improves. If the system is failing — recyclables in WTE, hazardous waste in general bins, valuable metals going to landfill — the divers profit from the failures AND report them. The programme grows in exactly the conditions where it's needed most.

### 4.5 Annual Diver Census

The NEB publishes an annual **Diver Recovery Report** — aggregate statistics (no individual identity) on:

- Total tonnes recovered by licensed divers
- Material breakdown (metals, electronics, reusables, etc.)
- Regional distribution of recovery
- Number of waste-hierarchy violation reports filed
- Estimated value of recovered materials

This report serves as a **shadow audit of the entire waste system.** If diver recovery is rising, the formal system is failing to capture materials it should. If diver recovery is falling, the formal system is working. The report is published alongside the NEB's own waste-stream data, creating a cross-check that neither the NEB nor the collection co-ops can manipulate — because the divers are independent of both.

## Section 5: Landfill — The Residual

### 5.1 Inert-Only Landfill

Only **inert materials** (construction rubble, ceramics, certain treated residuals) may be landfilled. No organic material. No combustible material. No hazardous material. No recyclable material.

Landfills are **state-owned, co-op-operated** (same as collection, §1.3). Each region maintains at least one engineered landfill facility with:

- Liner and leachate collection (even for inert materials — belt and suspenders)
- Groundwater monitoring wells
- Closure and post-closure plans funded by a **landfill closure reserve** (same locked-fund pattern as the Utilities Act renewal fund — users pay in, the fund covers closure, no politician can raid it)
- Progressive rehabilitation — closed cells are revegetated and returned to parkweb or agricultural use where feasible

### 5.2 Landfill Tax

A per-tonne landfill tax (set by the NEB, published annually) makes landfill the most expensive waste disposal option — reinforcing the hierarchy by pricing:

- **Recycling:** zero tax + zero profit tax + at-cost utilities = cheapest option
- **WTE:** modest gate fee (covers ash processing) = middle option
- **Landfill:** landfill tax + gate fee = most expensive option

The tax revenue funds the landfill closure reserves and the dumpster diver programme's administrative costs (~$2M/year nationally — the NEB's cost of issuing 25,000 licences).

## Section 6: Packaging Standards

### 6.1 Mandatory Recyclability

All packaging sold or distributed in New Crossroads must be **recyclable through the domestic recycling stream** within 5 years of enactment:

- **Immediately:** paper, cardboard, glass, aluminium, steel, PET, HDPE — all recyclable through existing MRF technology
- **Within 3 years:** multi-layer packaging (tetra pak, chip bags) must be redesigned for recyclability or face a non-recyclable packaging surcharge
- **Within 5 years:** no non-recyclable packaging may be sold. Imports that violate this standard are subject to a **packaging remediation tariff** equal to the estimated recovery cost

### 6.2 Deposit-Return System

All beverage containers (glass, aluminium, PET) carry a **refundable deposit** (~$0.10–$0.25/unit, DLB-indexed):

- Paid at purchase, refunded at return
- Return points are **dedicated bottle depots only** — small co-op operations licensed under the recycling FCL structure, receiving the full zero-tax, generation-cost utility, zero-property-tax advantage (§1.2). Depots are sited by Regional Boards to ensure coverage (~1 per 5,000–8,000 residents). Grocery and retail co-ops do not collect containers — they sell beverages, not waste. Keeping the streams separate prevents cross-contamination and keeps retail co-ops focused on their core function
- Depot co-ops process, sort, and bale returned containers on-site before forwarding to material-specific recycling co-ops. The depot is the **first link in the recycling chain**, not the last link in the retail chain
- Unredeemed deposits fund depot co-op operations and recycling co-op processing
- Target: **95%+ return rate** (comparable to Germany's 98% return rate on deposit bottles and Alberta's 85%+ rate through dedicated depots)

## Section 7: Funding and Governance

### 7.1 Funding Model

| Component | Funded by |
|---|---|
| Collection infrastructure (trucks, bins, MRFs) | **General revenue** — capital construction; access-fee recovery on operation (155% of maintenance, same as all state infrastructure) |
| Recycling co-op operations | **Self-funding** — material sales revenue + EPR levies + zero-tax advantage. The industry pays for itself |
| WTE operations | **Energy Act** — the WTE plants are energy infrastructure |
| Ash processing (aggregate, vitrification) | **Self-funding** — aggregate sales + metal recovery revenue. Vitrification costs funded from WTE gate fees |
| Landfill operations | **Landfill tax + gate fees** — self-funding with closure reserve |
| Diver programme administration | **Landfill tax revenue** (~$2M/year) |
| R&D (chemical recycling, advanced sorting) | **General revenue R&D allocation** (Sovereign Functions Act §7.3, ~$30B national R&D budget — waste tech is a named research priority) |

### 7.2 Governance

- **NEB** oversees waste policy, sets targets, audits performance, publishes recovery data, issues diver licences
- **Regional Boards** coordinate collection logistics, facility siting, inter-municipal waste flows
- **Co-op concessions** operate collection, sorting, and recycling — performance-contracted with recovery-rate bonuses and contamination penalties
- **Special Auditors** prosecute hazardous-waste violations, EPR fraud, and waste-hierarchy violations reported by divers or the NEB
- **Municipal councils** manage local collection schedules and bin placement — the closest-to-citizen level of waste governance

## Design Philosophy (Informative)

**Waste is a material stream, not a disposal problem.** The old regime landfilled everything — a mountain of lost value growing every year. This act treats waste as **feedstock that hasn't found its destination yet.** Metals return to smelters. Organics return to soil. Glass returns to furnaces. Ash returns to construction. The irreducible toxic fraction returns to the most secure containment on Earth. Nothing is "thrown away" because there is no "away."

**The dumpster diver is the system's conscience.** Every formal audit can be captured — the NEB might miss a violation, the collection co-op might ignore one, the regional board might deprioritize enforcement. The 25,000 licensed divers cannot be captured because their incentive is personal and permanent: they profit from the system's failures. A bureaucracy that polices itself will eventually stop. A population of 25,000 independent entrepreneurs who earn more money when the system fails will never stop looking.

**Zero tax isn't charity — it's recognition.** The recycling industry produces the highest positive externality of any sector in the economy: every tonne recycled is a tonne not mined, not combusted, not landfilled. The zero-tax treatment doesn't cost the state revenue — it costs the state the *absence* of a negative externality. The industry that prevents the most damage pays the least. That's not a subsidy. That's accounting done honestly.

**The ash loop closes the circle.** The WTE plant burns waste that can't be recycled and produces energy (5 GW). The ash becomes construction aggregate for Durahomes. The CO2 from combustion is captured by the pipeline network and used to cure the aggregate — permanently sequestering the carbon. The hazardous residual is vitrified and seals the nuclear repository. The waste system feeds the energy system feeds the housing system feeds the carbon-capture system feeds the nuclear system. **Every output is someone's input.** That's not idealism — it's thermodynamics applied to economics.
